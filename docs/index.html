Physicists have achieved a groundbreaking feat by precisely manipulating the magnetic orientation of a single proton within an atom's nucleus, an experiment previously considered 'impossible.' This involves 'flipping' a magnetic switch deep within the atom's core. Atoms consist of a nucleus (protons and neutrons) surrounded by electrons, both possessing a magnetic 'spin.' While electron spins are routinely controlled in quantum technologies, nuclear spins are far more challenging to manipulate due to their minuscule size and shielding by electron clouds. This success challenges existing quantum mechanics theories and opens new frontiers for quantum technology. It could pave the way for more robust quantum computing qubits, which rely on stable quantum states, or lead to the development of ultra-sensitive magnetic sensors. The ability to control nuclear spins with such precision represents a significant leap in our understanding and harnessing of fundamental atomic properties.

BAE Systems' UK division has developed a novel "ghost radar" system capable of detecting and tracking airborne threats, such as drones, without emitting any radio signals of its own. This passive detection capability means the system remains undetectable by adversaries, offering a significant tactical advantage. Traditional radar operates by transmitting radio waves and analyzing their reflections, a process that inherently reveals the radar's presence. In contrast, this new technology likely employs passive coherent location (PCL) or similar techniques, which analyze existing ambient radio frequency signals or other environmental cues for disturbances caused by targets. This advancement is critical for modern defense and security, enabling covert surveillance and early warning against sophisticated airborne threats without compromising the detector's position, thereby enhancing national security capabilities.

arXiv:2611.21885v2 Announce Type: new Abstract: A language model is trained once and frozen. Making it better takes another training run, or a prompt, a harness or extra computation at inference that is gone once the task ends. We show a third way: to our knowledge for the first time, a language model rewrites its own weights by its own reasoning and becomes better, with no gradients and no training. We call this self-rewriting: the model is its own optimizer. In earlier work, a frontier model edited a much smaller one; here an open 27B model, given its weights, its graded answers and the ability to run itself, diagnoses its own failure and rewrites the weights behind it. It surpasses itself: its own edit makes it 6.6 points more accurate on problems it never saw. It keeps getting better: it tests its edits on practice problems and submits only what works, each edit improving on the last, and over eight consecutive sessions its accuracy only rises. It repairs a damaged copy of itself, like a broken robot that fixes itself without being rebuilt: the copy solves almost nothing, yet with no retraining and without ever reading the original weights, the model brings it back to over 80%
