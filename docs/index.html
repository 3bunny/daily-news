GAUSS: A Workload-Aware Analytical and Monte-Carlo Simulator for LLM Inference Serving

arXiv:2610.37989v1 Announce Type: new Abstract: Large language model (LLM) inference has become an operational workload that fleets must provision, monitor, and bill against latency service-level objectives (SLOs). A single throughput number cannot answer the questions operators actually face, because generative …

Read more
This research introduces GAUSS, a sophisticated simulator designed to predict the performance of Large Language Model (LLM) inference serving. LLM inference, the process of generating responses, is operationally complex due to its two distinct phases—prefill (processing the input prompt) and decode (generating subsequent tokens)—and optimization techniques like continuous batching. Traditional throughput metrics are insufficient for operators who need to provision resources, monitor service quality, and meet strict latency Service Level Objectives (SLOs). GAUSS addresses this by combining calibrated, phase-specific latency models with a queueing model and Monte-Carlo sampling. This allows it to accurately predict full distributions of metrics like time-to-first-token (TTFT) and time-between-tokens (TBT), as well as sustainable request rates. Crucially, GAUSS can reason about workloads, routing policies, and hardware configurations that have not yet been deployed, and it effectively captures critical tail latencies that impact user experience. This capability is vital for optimizing LLM deployments, ensuring cost-effective resource allocation, and maintaining reliable, high-performance AI services in production environments.

This paper tackles a significant challenge in 'world models'—AI systems that build and interact with simulated environments: their inconsistency when revisiting previously generated regions. The authors frame this as a context retrieval problem, drawing parallels to how Large Language Model (LLM) agents manage their internal memory. They introduce a new benchmark specifically designed to measure world model context retrieval, ensuring that results are not skewed by training data recall or nearby frames. Their key contribution is an 'agent harness' that implements context compaction for world models. This technique allows models to effectively retrieve and utilize information from beyond their standard 'context window' (the maximum amount of information they can process at once) without requiring retraining. By enabling more consistent and persistent memory of generated worlds, this research significantly enhances the utility of world models. This is crucial for developing more coherent and realistic AI-driven simulations, games, and potentially future AI assistants that need to maintain a stable understanding of their environment and past interactions over extended periods.

This can be challenging because past decisions record what was done …

Read more
This research proposes a framework, 'Arbitrator,' for responsibly integrating AI into critical decision-making processes, specifically focusing on deceased-donor kidney allocation. The core challenge is determining when an AI should make a decision, defer to a human expert, or flag concerning data patterns and deviate from historical practices. In organ allocation, nearly 30% of recovered kidneys are discarded, often because they are difficult to place through the standard waitlist. AI could identify these 'high-risk' kidneys for 'out-of-sequence' (OOS) allocation, potentially saving more lives. However, relying solely on historical data can perpetuate biases or lead to suboptimal outcomes, as past decisions reflect what was done, not necessarily what should have been done. The Arbitrator framework uses data from the Organ Procurement and Transplantation Network to build a system that recommends OOS allocation only when an organ is demonstrably at high risk of discard on the standard sequence. This approach balances the efficiency gains of AI with the ethical imperative for human oversight, ensuring that AI augments, rather than replaces, human judgment in high-stakes healthcare decisions, fostering trust and maximizing organ utilization.

This paper introduces DynaRev, a novel benchmark designed to evaluate 'web agents'—AI systems that interact with websites—under dynamic goal revision. Current benchmarks typically assess agents based on fixed, pre-specified goals, which doesn't reflect real-world user behavior where requirements often change mid-interaction. DynaRev features 181 tasks that incorporate various types of goal revisions (e.g., additive, unconditional overrides, runtime-conditioned overrides), delivered at task-defined 'semantic milestones.' It evaluates agents on correctness, responsiveness, and efficiency using 'trajectory-grounded evaluation,' which assesses the actual steps taken by the agent. Initial evaluations reveal that existing web agents struggle significantly with dynamic revisions, often only partially fulfilling the updated requirements. This highlights a critical gap in current AI capabilities. DynaRev provides an essential tool for researchers and developers to build and test more robust and adaptable web agents. Such agents are crucial for creating truly intelligent digital assistants that can handle the fluid and evolving nature of human-computer interaction, thereby enhancing their practical utility and user experience in complex web-based tasks.

This research introduces EnvOS, a framework for rigorously auditing 'computer-use agents'—Large Language Model (LLM)-based systems that interact with software—in dynamic environments where information can change mid-task. A significant challenge for these agents is 'stale information,' where a value they've seen becomes outdated before they act on it. EnvOS addresses this by providing a method to build and certify environments that accurately simulate such changes, ensuring that environmental updates fire correctly, tasks remain solvable, and grading is precise. The framework also includes mechanisms to re-check evaluation verdicts using archived evidence. Applying EnvOS to scenarios like clinic booking and insurance claim approval, the study demonstrates that agents like Claude Opus 5 perform poorly when encountering stale information unless the updated value is explicitly shown. Simply reminding the agent to re-check is less effective. This work is crucial for developing reliable and safe AI agents, as it provides a robust methodology to test their resilience against real-world dynamism. Understanding these limitations helps design better strategies for agents to handle evolving information, making them more trustworthy for practical applications.
