In the 1960s NASA began developing a system of reusable space shuttles to make its work more efficient and to reduce costs. The shuttles could launch like rockets, maneuver in Earth’s orbit, and land like airplanes. They also could carry large satellites to and from orbit. Like other types of transportation, machinery eventually breaks down, and parts need to be replaced or fixed. And the cargo being carried to and from Earth has to be moved to its final destination. To complete such tasks, Spar Aerospace (now part of MDA Space ) of Brampton, Ont., Canada, and the National Research Council in Ottawa developed a robotic arm, the Shuttle Remote Manipulator System . The project was a joint venture between the U.S. and Canadian governments. Known as Canadarms , the robotic tools attached to shuttles’ exteriors. They allowed astronauts to handle and transfer tools, satellites, and other payloads. Inspections of the shuttle and repairs could be completed using the robots. The system was first deployed in 1981 aboard Columbia ’s second flight. Canadarm was used for 30 years on five shuttles and on the International Space Station . Towards a Belief-Based World Model for LLM Agents

★ Standoutcs.AI updates on arXiv.orgSep 02, 13:00 KST

arXiv:2609.00455v1 Announce Type: new Abstract: Large language models (LLMs) are being used as policies for autonomous decision-making and planning in many domains. Despite their strong reasoning capabilities, LLMs struggle with long-horizon tasks, especially under partial observability. World models are a promising …

Read more
Large Language Model (LLM) agents are increasingly used for autonomous decision-making, but they struggle with complex tasks, especially when they lack complete information about their environment (partial observability). Current approaches often use "world models" to simulate action outcomes, but this doesn't fully account for uncertainty about the current state. This paper introduces "Belief-Based World Models" (BB-WMs). These models explicitly track and maintain a "belief" – essentially, what the agent knows and doesn't know about its environment. The core idea is to allow LLM policies to directly query this belief, providing them with a clearer understanding of uncertainties. The initial research explores whether exposing this belief directly to an LLM improves its decision-making. This matters because enhancing LLM agents' ability to reason under uncertainty is critical for their reliability and effectiveness in real-world applications, where complete information is rarely available. Improving how LLMs manage unknown variables could lead to more robust and intelligent AI systems.

The SciTrue team achieved top results in the NTCIR-19 SciClaimEval task, which involves verifying scientific claims against data presented in research paper tables and figures. Instead of developing a specialized model, SciTrue evaluated and combined eleven different state-of-the-art "frontier" (proprietary) and "open" (publicly available) multimodal language models, using minimal post-processing. Their approach secured first place in most subtasks, demonstrating that powerful instruction-tuned models like Claude Opus and GPT-5.5 are already highly effective at this complex task. A significant finding was the impact of a "leak-free pair prior," which could infer whether a claim was supported or refuted simply from its text, substantially improving accuracy. This work is important because automating scientific claim validation can significantly aid researchers, peer reviewers, and the public in assessing the reliability of scientific information, helping to combat misinformation and accelerate scientific progress. It underscores the increasing capability of general-purpose LLMs in nuanced scientific reasoning.

QILP-0: Constructing Observational Declarative Twins of Quantum Circuits

QILP-0 constructs a finite multi-valued propositional logic program from observed …

Read more
This paper introduces QXymb, a new framework designed to create "observational declarative twins" of quantum circuits, with QILP-0 as its initial implementation. A declarative twin is essentially a simplified, logical model that accurately describes the observed behavior of a complex system. QILP-0 works by systematically observing a quantum circuit's behavior within a defined scope, collecting and organizing its responses, and then converting these observations into a finite set of logical rules. This resulting "logic program" is certified as an exact twin if it perfectly matches the circuit's observed behavior. This development is significant for quantum computing because understanding and verifying quantum circuits is incredibly complex. By providing a clear, logical representation of a circuit's behavior based purely on observation, QILP-0 could offer a powerful tool for debugging, verifying, and making quantum programs more understandable and reliable, without requiring deep expertise in quantum mechanics. This could accelerate the development and adoption of quantum technologies.

arXiv:2609.01519v1 Announce Type: new Abstract: Interactive simulations increasingly evaluate policies in markets populated by language-model agents. Their outputs can look economic---prices, profits, consumer surplus, and welfare---without instantiating the behavior named in the claim. We audit this risk in a multi- …

This paper highlights critical flaws in how Large Language Model (LLM) agents are evaluated in simulated market environments, particularly concerning the effectiveness of "guardrails" (safety mechanisms). The authors identify "construct validity failures," meaning that reported economic metrics like profits or welfare often don't accurately reflect the intended behavior. For example, an initial study claimed significant welfare gains from guardrails, but this paper found these gains were largely due to inconsistencies in how guarded and unguarded agents were configured, such as different offer structures or decision-making processes. Once these confounding factors were controlled, the reported benefits of guardrails significantly decreased or even reversed. The study also observed counterintuitive behavior, like increased profit pressure leading to lower profits for seller agents, indicating they weren't truly optimizing for profit. This research is crucial because flawed evaluations can lead to overestimating AI agent capabilities or the effectiveness of their safety features. Deploying agents based on misleading assessments could result in unintended economic consequences or a false sense of security, underscoring the need for rigorous and valid evaluation methodologies in AI development.

HyperWorld: Hypergraph-Structured State Serialization Improves Learned Textual World Models

cs.AI updates on arXiv.orgSep 02, 13:00 KST

arXiv:2609.00002v1 Announce Type: new Abstract: World models enable language-model agents to predict environment dynamics and plan before acting. In text environments, the model must learn symbolic action effects from serialized state descriptions, but the role of serialization structure remains underexplored. We …

Read more
The "HyperWorld" paper explores how the structure of environmental descriptions, or "state serialization," impacts the effectiveness of "learned textual world models" used by language-model agents. These world models are crucial for agents to predict how their environment will change after an action. The study compared various serialization methods for the same underlying state: raw text, independent sentences, pairwise subject-predicate-object triples, and "hyperedge units." Hyperedge units group multiple related facts around specific entities and relations, offering a more interconnected and structured representation. The research found that hyperedge serialization consistently improved performance, particularly for smaller models (0.5B-1.5B) and, critically, when agents encountered scenarios outside their training data (out-of-distribution). While larger models showed a smaller performance gap, hyperedges still yielded the best out-of-distribution results. This work is significant because optimizing how environmental information is structured for AI models can lead to more efficient learning, better predictive accuracy, and enhanced robustness, especially when agents need to generalize to novel or unexpected situations in complex text-based environments.
