Naval defenseTWZAug 09, 03:50 KST

Russia is implementing new defensive measures for its nuclear ballistic missile submarines, including cocooning them in nets. This action is likely a response to growing threats from uncrewed systems, aiming to protect critical naval assets at their bases.

Read more
Russian naval bases are deploying protective nets around their nuclear ballistic missile submarines (SSBNs), a move indicating a heightened concern over emerging uncrewed threats. These nets, observed at various locations, are a defensive measure designed to safeguard Russia's most critical naval assets. This development matters significantly because SSBNs are the cornerstone of Russia's nuclear deterrent, providing a "second-strike" capability that ensures retaliation even if land-based nuclear forces are neutralized. Protecting these high-value submarines is paramount to maintaining strategic stability and the credibility of Russia's nuclear posture. The deployment of physical barriers suggests that Russia perceives a tangible and growing threat from unmanned underwater vehicles (UUVs) or unmanned surface vessels (USVs), which could potentially target or compromise these submarines while docked. The context for this action likely stems from recent conflicts, particularly the war in Ukraine, where naval drones have proven effective in challenging conventional naval power. Ukraine has successfully employed both surface and underwater drones to attack Russian warships and infrastructure in the Black Sea, demonstrating the vulnerability of even well-protected naval assets to these relatively inexpensive and expendable systems. By cocooning its SSBNs in nets, Russia is adopting a practical, albeit low-tech, solution to mitigate these evolving threats, ensuring the continued security and operational readiness of its strategic nuclear forces against a new generation of naval warfare challenges.

The news reports a mathematical breakthrough: the discovery that "magic hexagons" exist for every possible order. Magic figures, like the more commonly known magic squares, are arrangements of numbers in a grid where the sum of numbers in each row, column, and specific diagonals (for squares) or lines (for hexagons) is constant. While magic squares have been studied for centuries and exist in various orders, magic hexagons, particularly "normal" ones using consecutive integers, have been considered extremely rare, with only one known example of order 1. This new finding likely refers to a broader definition or a novel construction method for magic hexagons, demonstrating their existence across all orders. This matters because it represents a significant advance in combinatorial mathematics and number theory. Such discoveries, while seemingly abstract, contribute to our fundamental understanding of mathematical structures and can sometimes find unexpected applications in fields like computer science, cryptography, or physics, by providing new tools for problem-solving or inspiring further research into patterns and symmetries.
