The U.S. Navy recently employed "one-way attack surface drones," akin to kamikaze vessels, during a SINKEX (Sink Exercise) off Hawaii. These uncrewed surface vehicles (USVs) detonated at the waterline of the decommissioned USS Peleliu, contributing to its sinking after other missiles had struck. This event marks a significant step in the U.S. Navy's adoption of attritable, uncrewed systems for offensive roles. It directly reflects lessons from the Ukraine war, where Ukraine has effectively used similar USVs to damage or sink Russian warships, demonstrating their potential to challenge larger, more expensive conventional navies. The deployment signals a strategic shift towards integrating cost-effective, high-risk platforms into naval warfare, enhancing capabilities for future conflicts and potentially reducing risk to human personnel. SINKEXs are routine exercises to test new weapons and tactics, and this particular exercise underscores the growing importance of autonomous systems in modern military doctrine.

The U.S. Coast Guard (USCG) is actively seeking advanced technologies to detect, track, and neutralize Uncrewed Underwater Vehicles (UUVs). This initiative stems from increasing concerns about the potential threat UUVs pose to critical maritime infrastructure, port facilities, and ships within harbors. UUVs, which can operate covertly, could be used for reconnaissance, sabotage, or even to deliver explosive payloads, presenting a complex security challenge that traditional maritime defenses may not adequately address. The USCG's pursuit of "hunting and killing" capabilities indicates a need for proactive, offensive counter-UUV measures, not just passive detection. This development highlights the growing sophistication and accessibility of UUV technology, which, while having legitimate uses, also presents a significant risk of malicious deployment by state or non-state actors. Protecting vital underwater assets and ensuring port security are key components of the USCG's mission, making this a critical area of focus.

New images have provided clearer insights into the demonstrator jet being developed by the UK, which will inform the design of its future Tempest stealth fighter. This demonstrator is a crucial component of the UK's ambitious Future Combat Air System (FCAS) program, aimed at developing a next-generation combat aircraft. The unveiling signifies tangible progress in the UK's commitment to maintaining a leading edge in advanced air combat technology. Developing a stealth fighter involves overcoming immense technological hurdles in aerodynamics, radar signature reduction, and sensor integration. The demonstrator allows engineers to test and refine these complex concepts in a real-world environment before committing to full-scale production, thereby mitigating risks and optimizing costs. This program, part of the Global Combat Air Programme (GCAP) with Italy and Japan, underscores the importance of international collaboration in developing cutting-edge defense capabilities to counter evolving global threats and ensure air superiority in future conflicts.
