In the Noisy Intermediate-Scale Quantum (NISQ) era, although quantum neural networks offer advantages in …

Read more
This research introduces a novel quantum incremental learning framework designed to enable quantum models to continuously learn new categories without "catastrophic forgetting"—the tendency to lose previously acquired knowledge when new information is introduced. A key challenge in quantum machine learning, especially during the current "Noisy Intermediate-Scale Quantum (NISQ)" era, is hardware limitations, particularly restricted circuit width (the number of qubits). Traditional quantum classifiers often struggle to accommodate a growing number of categories without expanding their quantum circuits. The proposed framework addresses this by using "trainable mixed-state prototypes." Instead of increasing the circuit width of the quantum backbone for each new class, new classes are incorporated by adding these prototypes. Mixed-state prototypes are significant because they offer richer information representation capabilities compared to simpler "pure-state" prototypes. Their decomposable nature also contributes to lower computational costs and provides a convenient "Hilbert-Schmidt distance" for classification. This innovation is crucial for developing practical, scalable quantum machine learning applications that can adapt and learn over time within the constraints of present and near-future quantum hardware.

Recent models can generate them from data and natural-language goals, but evaluating their usefulness remains difficult. Dashboard generation is open-ended, and …

Read more
This research introduces "DashArena," a novel benchmark designed to evaluate the capability of Large Language Models (LLMs) in generating interactive analytic dashboards. While LLMs are increasingly used to create dashboards from natural language descriptions, assessing the quality of these complex, open-ended outputs has been challenging. Existing evaluation methods often fall short by focusing only on static appearance or basic functionality. DashArena's key innovation is requiring LLMs to generate not only the dashboard itself but also a "replayable interaction trajectory"—a sequence of intended user actions. This trajectory is then executed in a browser, providing reproducible visual and functional evidence of the dashboard's analytical workflow. A Vision-Language Model (VLM) acts as a judge, comparing different LLM-generated dashboards based on this evidence, with results aggregated using the Bradley-Terry method. The researchers also developed "DashJudge-8B," an open-weight VLM judge that effectively mirrors human judgments. This benchmark is crucial for objectively measuring and advancing LLMs' ability to create truly useful and interactive data exploration tools, moving beyond superficial generation to assess deep analytical support and user experience.

This research introduces "ChemWorld," a novel programmable digital environment designed for autonomous chemistry experimentation. The goal is to provide a platform where AI agents can repeatedly interact with chemical processes, observe outcomes, and adapt their strategies. This addresses significant limitations of current approaches: physical laboratories are expensive and difficult to precisely repeat or control for specific interventions, while most existing digital environments have fixed underlying rules. ChemWorld's innovation lies in its flexibility. Researchers can programmatically define and vary the "world composition" (e.g., available chemicals, materials) and "operating conditions." Crucially, it allows for altering hidden chemical or material laws while keeping the agent's public task and interaction interface consistent. Furthermore, ChemWorld records every "transactional execution"—operations, failures, resource changes, and state transitions—enabling complete and exact replay of experiments. This ensures unprecedented reproducibility and auditability, accelerating the development and testing of AI agents for chemical research by providing a controlled, cost-effective, and highly customizable experimental platform.

This research identifies and characterizes a novel problem in "agentic coding," termed "catastrophic remembering," where instruction files for AI coding agents (like CLAUDE.md) grow indefinitely within software repositories. Unlike "catastrophic forgetting," where models lose old knowledge, this phenomenon describes an inability to efficiently discard outdated instructions. The core issue is that adding new instructions is simple, but deleting old ones becomes exponentially difficult and risky once their original rationale is lost, potentially breaking the agent's functionality. Analyzing instruction lifecycles across nearly 2,000 repositories, the study found that agentic prompts more than triple in size over their lifetime, accumulating approximately five new instructions per commit, with older instructions becoming increasingly entrenched. This unbounded growth leads to inefficient, costly, and difficult-to-manage prompts for AI agents. A significant finding is that incorporating "prompt comments" that encode the latent reasoning behind instructions can dramatically mitigate this growth, reducing excess instructions by over 99% in controlled environments. This research not only highlights a critical challenge in the long-term maintenance and efficiency of AI agents but also offers a practical, structured approach to prompt engineering to address it.

arXiv:2606.12826v2 Announce Type: replace-cross Abstract: Moving instance segmentation (MIS) attracts increasing attention due to its broad applications in traffic surveillance, autonomous driving, and animal tracking. Event cameras record asynchronous brightness changes, providing high temporal resolution and …

Read more
This research introduces "DIMOS," a novel framework aimed at improving Moving Instance Segmentation (MIS)—the task of identifying and precisely outlining individual moving objects within a scene. MIS is critical for applications such as autonomous driving and traffic surveillance. The method focuses on effectively integrating data from two distinct sensor types: traditional image cameras and "event cameras." Event cameras are unique as they record asynchronous brightness changes, offering high temporal resolution and dynamic range, making them highly sensitive to motion. A key challenge in current multimodal MIS methods is their struggle to segment small moving instances and the entanglement of appearance and motion cues within event camera data. DIMOS addresses this with a "dual-disentangling feature extraction framework" that explicitly separates appearance and motion information from both image and event modalities, thereby enhancing feature clarity and density. Subsequently, a "multi-granularity cross-modal alignment" mechanism is employed to ensure that these disentangled features are consistently aligned across the different sensor types. This approach promises to significantly boost the accuracy and robustness of moving object segmentation, particularly for challenging scenarios involving small or rapidly moving objects, by leveraging the complementary strengths of both camera types more effectively.
