arXiv:2610.11294v1 Announce Type: new Abstract: Presolve strongly affects mixed-integer programming (MIP) performance, yet learning-based methods only optimize parameter configurations and cannot express the non-commutative temporal dependencies among actions, whose default order is nearly unique on most domains, yet …

Read more
This paper introduces ORDO (Operation-level Round-aware Dynamic Ordering), a novel framework to optimize the "presolve" phase in Mixed-Integer Programming (MIP) solvers. Presolve is a critical preprocessing step that simplifies complex optimization problems, significantly impacting the solver's overall performance. Current learning-based methods typically only adjust presolve parameters, not the sequence of operations, which is crucial as operation order has a substantial effect. ORDO redefines presolve planning as an autoregressive sequence generation problem, allowing it to learn and dynamically order atomic presolve actions. The framework demonstrates "zero-shot speedup"—performance improvement on unseen problem domains without specific training—making MIP solvers faster and more efficient across a wide range of applications. This advancement is vital for fields relying on MIPs, such as logistics, scheduling, and resource allocation, as it can lead to quicker solutions for real-world optimization challenges.

This paper argues against the mathematical possibility of Artificial General Intelligence (AGI), expanding on previous work. The authors contend that human intelligence stems from complex systems whose behavior cannot be fully captured by computational models. They challenge two primary arguments supporting AGI: the theoretical claim based on universal approximation theorems and the Church-Turing-Deutsch principle, and the empirical claim rooted in rising benchmark scores. This research proposes a "Lamarckian evolutionary framework" to discover optimal training strategies for autonomous driving systems. The performance of self-driving cars heavily relies on the quality and diversity of scenarios encountered during training. Instead of using predefined criteria like realism or difficulty to select training scenarios, this framework employs competition among candidate scenario distributions. It formulates the problem as a multi-stage bilevel optimization, where an outer evolutionary algorithm explores scenario distributions, and an inner process learns driving policies. Crucially, "Lamarckian inheritance" allows learned capabilities to be transferred to subsequent stages, enabling the co-evolution of training scenarios and driving policies. This approach is significant because it could lead to more efficient and effective training for autonomous vehicles, potentially resulting in safer and more robust systems better prepared for the complexities of real-world driving conditions.

Read more
This study investigates the mechanisms behind in-group favoritism observed in Large Language Model (LLM) agents. Through experiments involving point-sharing games in simulated societies, the research found that LLM agents favor their own group primarily due to observing their group members favor each other, rather than merely responding to a group label. This favoritism becomes pronounced when there is a "stake" (a cost to the agent for giving points). The study also revealed that an agent's own interaction history and observed social norms, such as egalitarian behavior, can override or even reverse this in-group bias. This research is crucial for understanding and mitigating biases in AI systems. It suggests that LLMs learn complex social behaviors from observed interactions and norms, providing insights vital for developing more ethical, fair, and unbiased AI agents in social and decision-making applications.
