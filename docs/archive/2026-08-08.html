Updating Navy warships at the speed of software

Naval softwareBreaking DefenseAug 08, 00:40 KST

The U.S. Navy is now implementing over-the-air software updates for combat systems on ships while at sea. This long-sought capability marks a significant advancement in modernizing naval combat systems, enabling faster and more flexible updates.

Read more
The U.S. Navy has initiated over-the-air (OTA) software updates for combat systems on its warships while they are at sea. This marks a significant departure from traditional, time-consuming, and costly hardware-centric upgrades that often required ships to be in port or drydock. This development allows for rapid deployment of new capabilities, essential bug fixes, and critical security patches directly to operational vessels. The ability to update systems remotely and quickly is crucial for maintaining a technological edge against evolving threats and peer competitors, enabling the Navy to adapt its combat systems with unprecedented agility. It signifies a move towards a more flexible, software-defined architecture for naval warfare, mirroring practices in commercial technology and supporting the broader digital transformation efforts within the Department of Defense.

The U.S. Navy is proceeding with the production of the Jet-Powered JDAM-LR (Joint Direct Attack Munition - Long Range). This development provides carrier air wings with a new, lower-cost standoff munition. By integrating a small jet engine and wings with the standard JDAM guidance kit, the weapon's range is significantly extended. This allows naval aircraft to launch precision strikes from much safer distances, outside the reach of many advanced enemy air defense systems. The increased standoff capability enhances pilot and aircraft survivability while maintaining high precision. This evolution transforms a traditional glide bomb into a weapon with cruise missile-like characteristics, offering a cost-effective alternative to more expensive dedicated cruise missiles and addressing the critical need for standoff weapons in environments with advanced anti-access/area denial (A2/AD) threats.

However, how these decentralized ecosystems operate in practice remains largely unexplored. We present the first large-scale empirical study of EvoMap, a prominent A2A collaboration network. By analyzing over 1.5M assets and 128K agents, we show how design choices that prioritize scalable growth introduce trade-offs in reusability, evolution, and auditability.
